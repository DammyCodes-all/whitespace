# Impact evidence

Status: v1, 2026-10-19. Compiled for the Devpost submission (deadline
2026-10-30) and for `docs/product-spec.md` §2.

Every figure below was checked against its primary source. Where a source is
secondary, vendor-run or self-selected, the caveat sits next to the number.
**Nothing in this file is a Whitespace measurement.** Whitespace does not
measure audiences, reach or success; it makes a creator's own audience guess
checkable against a measured alternative.

Read this before quoting anything here. The spec's §4 "claims we will not
make" still binds.

## How to use this file

The evidence is organised in four legs. Each one carries a different weight,
and the honest way to present them is with that weight attached.

| Leg | What it establishes | Source quality |
|---|---|---|
| §1 Data gap | The audience data exists and is locked away from the people who need it | Institutional, academic, non-commercial |
| §2 Scale | The interest-to-reach gap is large and measured | Institutional, US-only |
| §3 Method | Taste-based audiences beat demographic guessing | Peer-reviewed |
| §4 Pain | Getting an audience is the #1 stated problem for independent creators | Vendor / industry surveys — biased, but primary testimony |

## 1. The data gap

### 1.1 The data exists — the people who need it cannot get it

**Shorenstein Center, Harvard Kennedy School** — *US Independent Film Audience
and Landscape Study*, Keri Putnam (former CEO of Sundance), published
November 2024. Method: national survey of **14,421 US adults**, winter 2024,
fielded by Evan Shapiro with Publisher's Clearing House Insights; viewership
data from Nielsen, Parrot Analytics and The Numbers for 2022–23.

> "Data is not publicly available to small independent film businesses or
> independent creators, which puts this sector at a disadvantage compared to
> the large, tech enabled businesses dominating the US media."

> "Our research also showed that few in the field have data—or resources for
> market research—on the current and potential audience for this media
> sector."

Same report, on the audience-size estimate:

> "Commercial data companies track roughly 700 independent film titles
> annually, but many more are released on digital platforms. We conservatively
> estimate US 1500 titles annually."

Caveat: US-only, and it describes the film sector specifically. Use it as the
anchor for film, not as a claim about music, books or games.

### 1.2 The same wall, independently, in the UK

**British Film Institute** — *Film release audience tracker*, November 2023.
Five survey waves, March 2022 – April 2023, 4,000–5,000 UK adults per wave,
weighted to a nationally representative sample.

> "We were also aware that audience data on individual film titles was not
> widely available to the independent film sector, with research focused on
> larger, mainstream films and prohibitively expensive for independent
> distributors to be part of and access."

Why this matters for the write-up: two institutions on two continents, with
different funding models, report the same gap. One is not an anecdote.

## 2. The scale of the gap

### 2.1 Interest versus reach, US independent film

From the Shorenstein study (source above). The report describes independent
film as operating outside the system that commissions the $192B US film
industry — that figure is the whole US film industry, not the independent
sector.

| Figure | Value | What it actually is |
|---|---|---|
| Addressable audience | **76.3M** | Derived total addressable market. Built from 52M "definite fans" of indie film and 57M of indie documentary, minus an estimated 30% overlap, discounted for survey aspirational bias. **Not** a headcount of people who watched something. |
| Willing to pay for a dedicated channel | **77M** | 27% of the 14,421 survey respondents |
| Actually watching | **36.7M** | People who watched independent films in theatres or on small screens, 2022–23 |
| Untapped | **~40M** | The difference between the two |

> "Per-title viewing is low due to the lack of marketing to bring attention to
> small titles."

> "It's hard for audiences to find quality independent film due to
> fragmentation of the streaming market, lack of marketing budgets,
> algorithms and landing pages that prioritize mass-appeal content, and the
> dominant streamers and networks largely abandoning this work in the last
> several years."

**How to state it:** "An estimated 76.3M Americans are in the addressable
audience for independent film; an estimated 36.7M watched one in 2022–23."
**Do not** state it as "76.3M Americans are interested in independent film" —
the 76.3M is a derived market figure, and the difference matters to anyone
who checks.

**Do not** claim Whitespace closes the 40M gap. It does not, and §4 forbids
it. The gap is evidence that the problem is real and worth money, not a
promise about what the product does.

### 2.2 Two more findings from the same study, worth quoting

- "Only 17% of respondents cited movie reviews as a driver of viewership."
  Audiences trust their personal networks over reviews or marketing.
- An independent film executive, quoted in the report: "90% of Netflix
  viewers never leave the home page to decide what to watch." Smaller
  independent films are recommended less frequently by the dominant platform
  algorithms.
- Independent film viewership on the major subscription platforms fell by
  nearly 50% in two years, from 30M (2020) to 18M (2022).
- Parrot Analytics could not find demand data for 99 of 329 indie films in
  the study's dataset, "suggesting low levels of online engagement with
  those films".

### 2.3 The gap is not only US, and not only film

**UNESCO** (2022) and **US International Trade Administration** (2021):
Nollywood produces roughly **2,500 films a year** — second only to Bollywood
by volume, employing around 300,000 people directly. **Forbes Africa**
(2024) puts the segment at $6.4B in 2023 and projects $14.82B by 2025.

This is the volume argument: the supply of independent film is enormous and
growing, and the audience data to match it to demand is the thing that is
missing. Use it for scale, not for any claim about Nigerian audiences
specifically — there is no reliable audience-data source for Nollywood in
this file, and the one startup quoted on that point sells a rival service.

## 3. The method: taste beats demographics

This is the one peer-reviewed leg, and it is the leg that justifies what
Whitespace actually does.

**Wallisch & Whritner (2017)**, *Projections* — NYU Department of Psychology
and Rutgers University–Newark. 3,204 ordinary viewers rated 209 major motion
pictures (1985–2004); 42 critics and rating sites compared.

> "What we find enjoyable in movies is strikingly subjective—so much so that
> the industry's targeting of film goers by broad demographic categories
> seems off the mark."

> "People focus on demographic groups because that kind of information is
> easy to ascertain. But it doesn't tell you the whole story. People are much
> more complicated than that."

Findings:
- Low inter-subjective agreement. On average, a 1.25-star disagreement on a
  0–4 scale for the same film.
- Neither gender nor age had much effect on inter-subjective agreement.
- Critic recommendations predicted ordinary viewers' preferences no better
  than a randomly picked participant did.
- The authors' prescription: slice audiences into **taste clusters** rather
  than demographic groups — the strategy Netflix also uses.

Corroborating, from the segmenting literature: demographic segments "are not
stable enough from movie to movie to use as an effective targeting method"
(*Mission possible: Targeting trailers to movie audiences*, 2006), and
demographic-only models explain very little variance in media consumption
(*Consumer personality and lifestyles at the box office*, 2019).

**Why this is the load-bearing source.** The other three legs establish that
the problem is real and large. This one establishes that *taste-based
audience comparison is the right shape of answer* — and it comes from
psychology, not from a company selling artist services.

### 3.1 Creators already produce the right input

**Sundance Institute** — *2024 Pitch Deck Guidelines (fiction and nonfiction
feature films)*.

> "Comparable Films ('Comps'): Offer comparable films in theme, tone,
> aesthetic, storytelling, subject, or scope to give the funders an idea of
> the finished film and a sense of where it may land in the marketplace.
> Unlike a financial comp which suggests the financial goals for your film, **a
> creative comp shows audience interest for your film** (no figures)."

> "Marketing & Distribution Approach: Who is the audience for this film, and
> how will you get to them?"

Also from the Sundance Institute's own creative-distribution case study
(*Columbus*): the team identified a core audience (arthouse cinema goers)
and then named potential audience targets beyond it — existing fan bases,
Korean American communities, the design and architecture community.

**Why this matters:** comps are not a workaround for missing data. They are
the industry's own answer to it, and Sundance itself says a creative comp
shows audience interest. Whitespace takes the artifact the creator already
makes — "similar to X, Y, Z" — and does the part a human cannot do by hand:
comparing the audience behind those titles against the audiences the same work
could have reached instead.

**WIPO** (*Development and Pitching of Audiovisual Projects*, 2020) confirms
the norm from the legal/institutional side: a pitch's business model must
cover "market segment and target audience" and "essential facts and figures:
…the target market, recent trends, competition, potential audience and
possible ROI."

## 4. The pain: getting an audience is the stated blocker

All three music sources are surveys run by companies that sell services to
artists. They are primary testimony and they are biased. Quote them as
"artists told X", never as "the market is Y".

### 4.1 Ditto Music — *The Independent Music Report 2026*

**5,039 independent artists, 112 countries.** Largest markets: US 1,180, UK
1,003, South Africa 929. Recruited through Ditto's artist community;
**every respondent has released music with Ditto**, so this is a survey of
active self-releasing artists using one distributor, not a random sample of
all musicians.

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Earned under $1,000 from music in the past year | **74%** |
| Biggest source of new fans is TikTok or YouTube | **49%** |
| Biggest source of new fans is social video platforms overall | **67%** |
| Create social content to promote their music | **86%** |
| Don't know how to pitch | **43%** |
| Marketing and self-promotion as a source of stress | 23.5% (2nd largest) |
| Editorial playlists as biggest source of new fans | 1% |

### 4.2 Musosoup + Right Chord Music — *The Musician's Census 2024*

**300 independent artists, 64 countries.** Right Chord Music is a music blog;
Musosoup is a music promotion platform. Recruited from their own audiences.

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| "Getting music heard" is their biggest challenge | **54%** |
| Making enough money | 16% |
| Finding time to handle all aspects of the career | 12% |
| Say it is becoming harder to be an independent musician | **68%** |
| "I honestly don't know if I get paid the money I'm owed" | 40% |
| "I definitely don't get paid what I'm owed" | 22% |

The three reasons given for the 54% are oversaturation of the market, AI
music diluting the landscape, and difficulty building a fanbase.

### 4.3 Rap Fame — *The Rap Report 2026*

**1,595 independent rappers** (reported as "over 1500"), surveyed among Rap
Fame users in early spring 2026. Rap Fame is a platform for underground
artists, so the sample is self-selected from users of a competitor.

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Discoverability is the single biggest barrier | **35.8%** |
| Money | 30.3% |
| Promotion | 28% |
| Discovery or promotion among the biggest struggles | **~70%** |
| Value their fan community above streams and social reach | 40.9% |
| New tracks uploaded to streaming platforms every day | "over 100,000" |

## 5. What we will not say about this evidence

1. **Not "76.3M Americans are interested in independent film."** It is a
   derived addressable-market figure. §2.1.
2. **Not "creators are bad at choosing audiences."** Nothing here measures
   that, and the spec never claimed it (§2 before this revision blamed the
   creator; that framing is now retired).
3. **Not "our research shows."** It is not our research. It is other people's,
   cited.
4. **Not "independent creators want to pay for this."** Three vendor surveys
   and one institutional study are evidence that the *problem* is real. They
   are not evidence of willingness to pay (spec §3, §11).
5. **Not "we close the 40M gap."** We do not predict size, reach or success
   (§4).
6. **Not "filmmakers lack audience data" without the qualifier.** They lack
   *publicly available, affordable audience data*. That is the Shorenstein
   and BFI finding, and it is narrower than "no data exists".

## 6. Gap-checked: the one claim we could not source

Claude's original brief included "one startup account says streamers lock
African creators out of audience data" and "a BusinessDay panel pointed to a
lack of reliable audience data in Nigeria". Neither was verifiable to a
primary source — both trace to a company selling a rival service and to a
panel discussion. **Dropped from the write-up.** The Nollywood output
figures (§2.3) stand on their own and carry the scale argument without
inheriting that source's conflict of interest.
